What Is the Difference Between PII and PHI When AI Agents Handle Sensitive Data

PII covers any data that identifies a person in any setting, whereas PHI exists only when identifiable health or payment-for-care information sits with a covered entity or business associate. The same name can be either, depending on who holds it.

PII: A Broad Privacy Category

PII has no single US federal definition. NIST describes it as information that can distinguish or trace a person's identity on its own or when combined with other data, and state privacy laws each draw the line a little differently.

For an AI agent, that means almost any customer conversation carries PII. Names and email addresses count, and so do indirect details like a ZIP code paired with a birth date. Our explainer on what counts as PII covers the categories in depth.

PHI: A HIPAA Category Defined by Who Holds the Data

PHI is a legal category under HIPAA. Information counts as PHI when three conditions are met:

  1. It relates to a person's health condition or the care they receive, including payment for that care
  2. It identifies the person or could reasonably be used to identify them
  3. Its holder is a covered entity or business associate

Covered entities include health plans and most healthcare providers, along with healthcare clearinghouses. A business associate is any vendor that handles PHI on their behalf, which can include the companies behind an agent's model or its logs.

How PII Becomes PHI as AI Agents Retrieve and Combine Sensitive Data

PII becomes PHI when a covered entity or business associate links it to health information or payment for care. Inside an AI agent this happens fast, because one retrieval can join a member's contact details to her claim and policy files.

Three triggers move data across the line:

  • Linking: an identifier joins health or payment-for-care information in the same context
  • Holding: the data sits with a covered entity or a vendor acting for one, while the same email in a retail loyalty program stays PII
  • Record context: contact details stored in the same member record as claims take on PHI protection

Why PII and PHI Create Different Risks When AI Agents Work With Them

PII and PHI create different risks because the consequences differ when an AI agent overreaches. Exposed PII is usually a privacy incident, while exposed PHI can become a reportable HIPAA breach that pulls in vendor contracts and regulators.

AI agents create new paths for sensitive information. Each of the three paths below looks the same technically for PII and PHI, but the stakes change once health data is involved.

Access Can Exceed the Task

Broad connector credentials let an agent reach records unrelated to the request in front of it. A claims connector that returns every member's file gives the agent far more PHI than Elena's question needs.

  • With PII: overreach exposes contact details and account history, which privacy laws and your own policies govern
  • With PHI: overreach can count as a use or disclosure beyond the minimum necessary, which HIPAA restricts directly

Information Spreads Through the Stack

Every copy of PHI brings HIPAA safeguards to the system that holds it. Agents create copies quickly, often in places nobody reviews:

  • Model providers: a provider that receives PHI is generally treated as a business associate and needs a BAA
  • Tracing and observability: prompt captures put PHI inside debugging dashboards
  • Agent memory: saved facts about a member's condition persist into later sessions
  • Vector stores: embedded claim notes keep clinical detail searchable long after the case closes

PII copies need protection too, and PII protection for LLMs covers the model side. With PHI, each copy also widens the set of vendors and systems that fall under HIPAA.

Outputs Become Business Decisions

A coverage answer is a decision the member acts on. If the agent says a procedure is covered when an exclusion applies, Elena may book care she ends up paying for herself.

Answers can also leak. A reply that quotes details from another member's file turns a wrong answer into a disclosure, which is why output checks matter more for PHI than for most PII use cases.

Why Tokenizing PII Does Not Automatically De-Identify PHI for AI Agents

Tokenizing PII in a prompt lowers exposure, yet it does not make PHI de-identified under HIPAA. Tokens can be reversed by an authorized system, and clinical free text often carries identifiers that a PII detector was never configured to catch.

The 18 HIPAA Identifiers and Why Clinical Text Slips Past Them

HIPAA's Safe Harbor method lists 18 identifiers that must be removed before health data counts as de-identified. Grouped by type, they look like this:

Identifier groupItems on the Safe Harbor list
Names and contact detailsNames; phone and fax numbers; email addresses
Location and timeGeographic units smaller than a state; all dates tied to a person except the year
Record and account numbersMedical record numbers; health plan beneficiary numbers; account numbers
Government and license IDsSocial Security numbers; certificate and license numbers
Device and online identifiersVehicle and device identifiers; URLs; IP addresses
Physical identifiersBiometric identifiers; full-face photos
Catch-allAny other unique identifying number, characteristic or code

Claim notes rarely follow a neat format. A date of service or a small town named in a sentence is an identifier under Safe Harbor, even though teams seldom think of it as personal data.

Safe Harbor vs Expert Determination

HIPAA offers two routes to de-identification. Both apply to the data set as a whole:

  • Safe Harbor: remove all 18 identifiers and have no actual knowledge that what remains could identify someone
  • Expert Determination: a qualified expert applies statistical methods and documents that the risk of re-identification is very small

Masking a name in a prompt meets neither standard. An agent request is a working context, and de-identification is a formal finding about a data set.

Why Tokens Stay Pseudonymous

A token maps back to its original value inside a vault, which makes tokenized data pseudonymous. That is useful for an AI agent, because an authorized application can restore Elena's email when it sends her reply.

The same reversibility means tokenized PHI should still be treated as PHI unless your privacy team has documented a de-identification method. Pseudonymization and anonymization differ on exactly this point.

How to Protect PII and PHI in AI Agents With Scoped Access and Verified Outputs

Protect PII and PHI in AI agents by scoping access to the task and tokenizing identifiers before model calls, then checking outputs against approved evidence. PHI adds two steps up front: confirming your HIPAA role and signing BAAs with each vendor.

These six steps follow one agent path from planning to the reply Elena receives. Steps 1 and 2 matter most when PHI is involved, while steps 3 to 6 apply to any sensitive data.

Step 1: Map the Agent Path and Your HIPAA Role

Start with one agent and answer three questions before changing anything:

  1. Does the agent handle PHI, or PII on its own?
  2. Is your organization a covered entity or a business associate for this task, or neither?
  3. Which systems receive data along the way, from the model provider to tracing and backups?

The answers decide which rules apply. They also show where protection belongs and which vendors need contracts.

Step 2: Confirm a BAA With Every Vendor That Receives PHI

Any vendor that receives PHI on your behalf needs a BAA for the exact service and deployment you use. In an agent's path, that usually means the model provider and the data protection layer, plus any observability vendor that captures prompts.

If a vendor won't sign, keep PHI out of its path. Tokenizing identifiers before they reach that vendor reduces exposure, though your compliance team still decides whether the remaining data counts as PHI.

Step 3: Scope Access to the Records the Task Needs

Elena's question needs her claim and the policy that governs it. Nothing else belongs in the agent's reach.

Align these decisions with your existing identity and application policy. For PHI, this step is how the minimum necessary standard shows up in practice.

Step 4: Minimize Exposure Before Every Model Call

Replace identifiers with tokens before the request reaches any model. The model can still reason about Elena's case because each token stays consistent across the conversation.

Test detection against real claim notes before launch. Personal identifiers are one part of PHI, so procedure details and dates of service need review as well, and any request that can't be tokenized should be blocked.

Step 5: Govern Release Outside the Model

Original values stay inside a protected boundary until an approved destination needs them, such as the claims system or the member portal. Release decisions follow application policy, so instructions hidden in a prompt can't unlock Elena's record.

This also limits damage if an agent is manipulated. On protected paths the agent holds references, and requests to use the underlying values are checked outside the model and logged. These controls reduce the consequences of a compromised agent without removing every prompt injection risk, so other agent defenses still apply.

Step 6: Check the Output Before It Reaches the Member

Check each statement in the agent's answer against the evidence it was allowed to use. Claim-level verification marks what the evidence supports, and your policy decides what happens to the rest.

A verdict measures support within the approved evidence. It doesn't certify truth beyond that evidence, which is why the policy and claim files themselves need to be current.
