Cross-border data sharing isn’t really about where the data goes. It’s about where the readable copy lives and who can turn a token back into a name from which country.

A US company receiving EU customer data, sending Americans’ data to a contractor abroad, or pushing prompts to an AI provider faces the same two questions from counsel: where is the original, and who can reach it?

This guide compares 6 platforms on exactly that: published vault regions, residency model, key control, and how well each fits the transfer laws a US company actually deals with (GDPR Chapter V, the EU-US Data Privacy Framework, standard contractual clauses, and the US Department of Justice’s Data Security Program).

What Is a Privacy Vault for Cross-Border Data Sharing?

A privacy vault for cross-border data sharing keeps readable personal data encrypted in a region you choose and hands out tokens, so teams abroad can use the data without holding the original.

Key Features to Look for in a Privacy Vault for Cross-Border Data Sharing

In any demo, these are the things worth checking first:

  • Published vault regions: A named list of where originals are stored, not a vague “global” claim.
  • Residency and sovereignty: Residency is where data physically sits; sovereignty is whose laws and which operators can reach it, and you need answers to both.
  • Key location and BYOK: Where the encryption keys live, and whether you can bring your own key (BYOK) from a key manager you control.
  • Detokenization by region: Only systems in the exporter’s region should be able to turn tokens back into real values, with every reveal logged.
  • Consistent tokens: The same input gets the same token, so partners abroad can still join, dedupe, and report.
  • Every copy accounted for: Backups, logs, and disaster-recovery replicas should sit in named regions too.
  • Deletion and expiry: Delete the original once and every token pointing to it becomes meaningless everywhere.
  • Pseudonymised, not anonymous: Under GDPR, tokenized data is still personal data (GDPR Article 4(5); EDPB Guidelines 01/2025).

Common Mistakes US Companies Make When Buying a Privacy Vault for Cross-Border Data

  • Treating tokens as anonymous data: Tokenized data is pseudonymised and still personal data under GDPR, and the DOJ rule covers encrypted data too.
  • Checking the primary region only: Backups, logs, and disaster-recovery copies can sit in another country, so ask for every copy’s region.
  • Ignoring who can call reveal: Residency means little if a support engineer abroad can detokenize at will; restrict reveal by system and region.
  • Relying on the Data Privacy Framework alone: It only covers certified recipients and could be challenged again, so keep SCCs and technical measures ready.
  • Forgetting AI providers: A prompt sent to a model API in another country is a transfer too, so tokenize before it leaves.

Smart Questions to Ask a Privacy Vault Vendor Before You Sign

  • Which regions can store our originals today, by name, and will you put that list in the contract?
  • Where do backups, logs, and disaster-recovery replicas live?
  • Who can detokenize, from which systems, and is each reveal logged?
  • Who holds the keys, and can we bring our own?
  • Which subprocessors touch our data, and in which countries?
  • How do your controls support our DOJ Data Security Program obligations, given encryption alone doesn’t exempt covered data?
