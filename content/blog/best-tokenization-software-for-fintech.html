Vault Tokens vs Network Tokens: The Decision That Shapes Your Stack

Most fintech teams meet two kinds of tokens in the same quarter, and vendors blur them. Getting this right decides whether you can switch processors, how much your partner bank can audit, and whether your authorization rates move.

A vault token is issued by your tokenization provider. It stands in for a card number, account number, or SSN inside your own databases, logs, and support systems. Only the vault can swap it back, and only for requests your policy allows. Its job is to keep plaintext out of your systems and to keep stored data portable across processors.

A network token is issued by a card network (Visa Token Service, Mastercard Digital Enablement Service) for one card and one merchant or device. It is used in the authorization itself, gets updated when a card is reissued, and can lift approval rates. It only applies to cards, and it is tied to your merchant relationship.

The two are not rivals. A lender pulling repayments from debit cards wants network tokens for approvals and a vault for the PAN, the bank account fallback, and the borrower’s SSN.

An issuing program wants neither in the usual sense: it needs a vault that can receive PANs from an issuer processor and reveal them to the cardholder without your servers seeing them. Decide which of those three jobs you have before you compare vendors.
